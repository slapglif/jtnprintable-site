Game room decorating guide

Moody Billiards Room Wall Art: Build a Focused Pool-Room Gallery

A billiards room already has a strong visual center: the table, pendant light, cue rack, and open space needed for play. Wall art works best when it reinforces that atmosphere without entering the cueing zone or making the room feel crowded. A coordinated set of dark pool-room prints can connect the green felt, wood rails, leather seating, and warm metal details while leaving the table as the main event.

Start with safe cue clearance

Measure from every table rail to the nearest wall and account for a full backswing. Do not place projecting frames where a cue tip, shoulder, or player can strike them. The safest walls are often behind spectator seating, above a shallow sideboard, or opposite the primary cue rack. Use paper templates cut to the outside frame dimensions before drilling.

  • Above a credenza: keep the gallery narrower than the furniture and leave enough clearance for lamps or scorekeeping accessories.
  • Behind seating: center the group on the bench or sofa rather than on the full wall.
  • On a narrow return wall: use two columns of three, but confirm the frames do not reduce the walking path.

Choose a club-inspired palette with restraint

Charcoal, deep green, tobacco brown, warm cream, oxblood, and aged-brass tones can create a classic club atmosphere. Repeat only two or three of these colors in the room. If the table felt is bright tournament blue or red, use quieter black, walnut, or dark bronze-tone frames so the gallery does not compete with it.

Matte paper and glazing with reduced reflections help under low pendant lights. Avoid glossy paper directly opposite a bright fixture. A generous cream or warm-white mat gives dark imagery breathing room, while a narrow black frame keeps the arrangement crisp.

Lay out six prints around the room architecture

A two-by-three grid is the most adaptable arrangement. Keep all gaps equal, align outer edges, and distribute the darkest designs instead of clustering them. Above a long sideboard, a single horizontal row can work if the finished width stays comfortably inside the furniture edges. For a taller wall beside a bar cabinet, two columns of three create a compact footprint.

Place the visual center near normal standing eye level, then adjust for nearby furniture. A gallery above seating should relate to the back of the bench; one above a sideboard should not float near the ceiling. Tape up paper templates and inspect the plan from the room entrance and from the shooting positions around the table.

Control lighting and reflections

The table light should illuminate play without washing out the wall. Test the gallery at night, when a game room is most likely to be used. If reflections obscure the prints, shift the frames laterally, adjust fixture direction, or choose matte glazing. Keep valuable prints away from damp basement walls and direct sunlight.

Plan printing before ordering frames

Measure the actual mat opening and select the supplied file prepared for that aspect ratio. Never stretch an image to fill a different opening. Review the print service crop preview around cue balls, table edges, lamps, and architectural details. Print one design first if you need to confirm color, paper, and scale.

Billiards room wall art FAQ

Where should it hang?

Choose a dry wall outside cueing and walking clearance, commonly above spectator seating or a sideboard.

What palette works?

Deep green, charcoal, walnut, warm cream, and restrained brass tones support a club-like room.

How should six prints be arranged?

Use a two-by-three grid, a long row above wide furniture, or two columns on a narrow wall.
